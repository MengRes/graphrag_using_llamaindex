# Arctic Climate Observatory: Research Program Report 2024-2025

## Chapter 1: Program Overview and Participating Institutions

The Arctic Climate Observatory (ACO) is an international research program established in 2020 to monitor and model climate change impacts across the Arctic region. The program is coordinated by the Norwegian Polar Institute in Tromsø and involves twenty-three research institutions from eight countries: Norway, the United States, Canada, Denmark (Greenland), Sweden, Finland, Russia, and Iceland.

Professor Ingrid Haugen of the Norwegian Polar Institute serves as Program Director. Dr. William Thornton of the National Snow and Ice Data Center (NSIDC) at the University of Colorado Boulder leads the sea ice monitoring division. Dr. Yuki Tanaka from the Japan Agency for Marine-Earth Science and Technology (JAMSTEC) coordinates the ocean-atmosphere interaction modeling team, despite Japan not being an Arctic nation.

ACO receives primary funding from the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) research fund ($45 million over five years), the European Union Horizon Europe program (€32 million), and the U.S. National Science Foundation (NSF grant ARC-2031847, $12 million). Additional support comes from the World Meteorological Organization and private foundations including the Gordon and Betty Moore Foundation.

The program operates twelve permanent research stations across the Arctic, from Ny-Ålesund in Svalbard (79°N) to Utqiagvik (Barrow) in Alaska (71°N). Mobile monitoring platforms include two icebreaker research vessels, RV Kronprins Haakon (Norway) and USCGC Healy (United States), and a fleet of autonomous underwater gliders.

## Chapter 2: Sea Ice Extent and Thickness Measurements

Sea ice monitoring is the longest-running and most data-rich component of the ACO program. Measurements span satellite remote sensing, airborne surveys, and in-situ buoy deployments.

Satellite data comes primarily from the Copernicus Sentinel-1 SAR constellation and NASA's ICESat-2 altimetry mission. Dr. Thornton's team at NSIDC processes daily sea ice extent maps at 25-kilometer resolution. The 2024 minimum Arctic sea ice extent of 4.12 million square kilometers (recorded on September 14, 2024) was the sixth-lowest in the 46-year satellite record. The long-term trend shows a decline of 13.2% per decade relative to the 1981-2010 baseline.

Ice thickness measurements combine ICESat-2 laser altimetry with submarine sonar data archived by the U.S. Navy and British Royal Navy. Professor Haugen's group published findings in Nature Climate Change (March 2025) showing that multi-year ice volume in the central Arctic has decreased by 58% since 2005. The study used GraphRAG-based literature synthesis to cross-reference 340 prior studies on ice thickness trends, ensuring comprehensive coverage of historical data sources.

Autonomous buoys deployed through the International Arctic Buoy Programme (IABP) provide ground-truth temperature, pressure, and drift data. In 2024, ACO maintained 112 active buoys, with data relayed via Iridium satellite to the Global Telecommunication System (GTS). Buoy data feeds into the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) assimilation system, improving Arctic weather prediction accuracy by an estimated 15%.

## Chapter 3: Permafrost Thaw and Carbon Feedback Loops

Permafrost monitoring addresses one of the most significant unknowns in climate modeling: the potential release of greenhouse gases from thawing Arctic soils.

ACO operates eight permafrost observatory sites across Alaska, Siberia, and northern Canada. Each site measures soil temperature at depths from 0 to 100 meters, active layer thickness, ground subsidence, and greenhouse gas fluxes using eddy covariance towers. Dr. Anya Volkov of the Permafrost Carbon Network at Northern Arizona University analyzes carbon flux data from these sites.

The 2024 annual report documents that active layer thickness increased by an average of 4.7 centimeters across all sites compared to 2023, continuing a statistically significant trend (p < 0.01) observed since measurements began in 2020. Methane emissions from thermokarst lakes in the Yamal Peninsula, measured by Dr. Volkov's team, exceeded previous model predictions by 22%.

GraphRAG technology plays an emerging role in permafrost research by connecting findings across disciplines. ACO researchers used NovaMind Labs' GraphRAG Engine to build a knowledge graph from 1,200 permafrost-related publications, identifying cross-study relationships between soil microbiome composition, thaw depth, and methane production rates. Community detection revealed three distinct research clusters: physical thaw processes, microbial carbon cycling, and remote sensing detection methods.

The permafrost carbon feedback remains one of the largest uncertainties in IPCC Sixth Assessment Report climate projections. ACO's data contributes directly to the IPCC AR7 preparation cycle, with Professor Haugen serving on the Working Group I drafting team.

## Chapter 4: Impact on Arctic Ecosystems and Indigenous Communities

Climate change impacts on Arctic biodiversity and the four million people who inhabit the region are documented through ACO's ecological and social science research divisions.

Dr. Lars Eriksson of the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences leads the terrestrial ecology team, monitoring vegetation shifts using satellite NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index) time series. The "greening of the Arctic" trend accelerated in 2024, with shrub expansion documented at seven of nine long-term vegetation plots. Caribou migration patterns tracked by GPS collars show a northward shift of winter grazing areas by an average of 12 kilometers per decade.

Marine ecology research led by Dr. Maria Santos of the Institute of Marine Research in Bergen, Norway, documents changes in Arctic fish populations. Atlantic cod have expanded their range northward, competing with native Arctic cod populations around Svalbard. Polar bear body condition indices, measured through aerial surveys by the Norwegian Polar Institute, show a 15% decline in average body mass around Svalbard since 2010, correlating with reduced sea ice hunting platforms.

Indigenous community engagement is coordinated by the Saami Council and the Inuit Circumpolar Council. ACO funds community-based monitoring programs in twelve villages across Alaska, Canada, and Greenland, where local observers record ice break-up dates, wildlife sightings, and coastal erosion using standardized protocols. Dr. Elena Kwon of the University of Alaska Fairbanks published a GraphRAG-assisted synthesis of indigenous knowledge records, integrating oral history data with instrumental measurements to extend the effective climate record by 60-80 years in some regions.

## Chapter 5: Policy Implications and 2026 Research Agenda

ACO research directly informs international climate policy through IPCC assessments, Arctic Council reports, and national climate adaptation plans.

The 2024 ACO synthesis report, presented at COP29 in Baku, concluded that the Arctic is warming at four times the global average rate (a phenomenon known as Arctic amplification). The report recommended immediate policy actions including stricter methane emission regulations, expanded protected areas for migrating species, and increased funding for indigenous community adaptation infrastructure.

Key findings presented to the Arctic Council included evidence that shipping traffic through the Northern Sea Route increased by 35% in 2024, raising concerns about black carbon deposition on sea ice and marine pollution. ACO modeling suggests that summer ice-free conditions in the central Arctic Ocean may occur by 2035 under current emission trajectories, earlier than the IPCC AR6 median estimate of 2050.

The 2026 research agenda prioritizes five areas: deployment of next-generation autonomous monitoring networks, integration of GraphRAG-based cross-disciplinary literature synthesis into all ACO working groups, expansion of permafrost monitoring to twenty sites, launch of the Arctic Carbon Dashboard (a public data portal), and initiation of a joint program with the Antarctic research community to compare polar climate feedback mechanisms.

Professor Haugen announced that ACO will host the International Arctic Science Conference in Tromsø in September 2026, expecting 2,000 participants from over fifty countries. NovaMind Labs has been invited to demonstrate GraphRAG Engine applications for climate science literature synthesis at the conference.
